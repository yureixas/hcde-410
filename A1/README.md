## Questions I asked

**Q1. When is the trail busiest, and when is it quietest?**

- Decide what *busy* means to you first — a single hour? a stretch of the day? bikes and peds together, or separately? — and say why you chose that.

**My Q1 answer:**

- *I decided to model when the trail is busiest by using a stretch of a day and seeing what hours are busiest. I combined bikes and pedestrians because they both contribute to how busy the trail is at a certain hour. With the graph, the hour when traffic is busiest is around 5 pm., and when it is quietest is from 1- 3 am. This makes sense in context, because most people are sleeping at 1-3am, but there may be multiple reasons why 5pm is the busiest hour during the daytime, like if most people end class at that time, which this graph can't currently explain.*


**Q2. Do cyclists and pedestrians use the trail at different times of day?**

- Compare their hourly patterns. If they differ, what might explain it?

**My Q2 answer:**

- *Based on the graph, it seems that pedestrians seem to decrease from 9 am to 5pm while cyclists increase at the same time range. Cyclists also seem more likely to use the trail compared to pedestrians, as the bike line is consistently above the peds line during active hours. What could likely explain this pattern could be multiple reasons, but my hypothesis is perhaps that the Burke-Gillman trail is cyclist-friendly, and there could be other routes pedestrians are using instead.*


**Q3. Commuting or recreation?**

- Is there evidence people use the Burke-Gilman to *commute*, not just for fun? Compare **weekdays vs. weekends**, and maybe morning vs. evening. Define what a "commuting pattern" would look like in the data, then say whether you see it.

**Your Q3 answer:**

- *Based on the graph, it seems like there is more activity on the weekend compared to weekdays. The "commuting pattern" would be that the graph shows either pedestrians or cyclists used the trail more on a given day of the week. The "commuting pattern" supported by this graph seems to be that both cyclists and pedestrians use the trail more on the weekends compared to weekdays, which could mean that people use the Burke-Gilman for fun.*

**Q4. Direction and flow.**

- The counter records **north vs. south** separately. Are mornings mostly one direction and evenings the other? What would that tell you about who's using the trail and where they're heading?

**Your Q4 answer:**

- *Based on the graph, it seems that more people are going north in the morning and more people are going south in the evening. I combined both cyclists and pedestrians again to see where the overall traffic was coming from. By looking up the map of the Burke-Gilman trail, the south is near the University of Washington and the north is near Bothell, where it also seems that there are a lot of parks north of the trail. I hypothesize that the majority of people using the trail are people living near the University of Washington are heading up north to go to these parks and return back home during the evening.*

**Limitations or bias of the data**

- *One bias this dataset could have that could mislead someone is if the people on the trail went back and forth on the trail multiple times. This could skew the data to have higher counts for cyclists or pedestrians in the set than there were in reality. This could mislead someone into thinking that more cyclists are using the path than there are and make wrong conclusions.*
